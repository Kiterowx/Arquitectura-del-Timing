# Resolve competing priorities

When reading time, speech, and the picture suggest different edges, an order of priorities helps resolve the conflict. Before moving anything, identify the problem: a clipped word, text that disappears too quickly, an awkward cut, or a distracting gap.

Readability comes first because a subtitle must be understood, but extra time can change when the viewer learns something. Bringing a reply forward may spoil the silence before it; holding it longer may compete with the next line. If neither edge has room to move, revise the wording or divide the thought where the dialogue supports it. Keep the meaning and tone of the exchange as you make room for the reading.

## Different edges need different attention

After a pause, check the next speech onset and its lead-in. At the end of a complete thought, the word ending, reading time, and picture determine the exit. Rapid dialogue needs particular attention to gaps and flicker while preserving enough time to read each cue. A sign is timed against its appearance in the picture, with reading time as a further constraint.

## Common conflicts

| Conflict | Suggested approach |
| --- | --- |
| The text needs more time than the shot allows | Try a clearer split or shorter wording before holding it far into the next shot. |
| A word continues across a cut | Cover the word; consider segmentation only if the thought also divides there. |
| A short gap represents a deliberate pause | Preserve it if closing it would change the performance. |
| Chaining would reduce reading time | Keep enough duration for the text and reconsider the shared edge. |
| Lead-out runs into a new shot | Use the cut if both the speech and reading are already complete. |

## Match the adjustment to the evidence

A late entry confirmed by listening is a stronger reason to move an edge than an unidentified peak on a waveform. A cue that repeatedly disappears before the reader can finish deserves attention; a high CPS value on its own needs context.

When the evidence is weak, inspect the passage again. A breath, a subtle cut, or a familiar short expression can make an automatic marker misleading. The result needs to work in playback, regardless of how tidy its numbers look.
