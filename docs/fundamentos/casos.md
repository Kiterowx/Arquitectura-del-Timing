# Timing decisions in practice

These cases start with an observable problem and show when to keep or change the boundary.

## A cue with room on either side

After a pause, start with the usual lead-in so the text is ready for the first syllable. Shorten it if it intrudes on the previous cue or reveals dialogue before the picture supports it. At the end of a complete thought, allow a hold for reading, then check whether a nearby cut offers a better exit.

## Speech near a cut

| What happens | Initial choice | What can change it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speech ends about 150 ms before a cut. | End on the cut. | Keep more reading time if the sentence needs it. |
| A quiet vocal tail continues about 150 ms past a cut. | Listen to that tail before moving anything. | A perceptually minor decay may allow an earlier exit; a word or new information must stay covered. |
| The first syllable starts just before a cut. | Cover the syllable. | Split only if the content also divides there. |
| A cut falls inside a continuous sentence. | Keep the cue across the cut. | A new speaker, thought, or focus may justify a split. |
| Lead-out alone spills into the next shot. | Shorten the hold to the cut. | Retain it if the reader still needs the time. |

## A short gap with a purpose

An 80 ms gap is short enough to flicker, but its length alone cannot establish whether the pause matters. Compare the chained version with the original rhythm. Keep the gap if it supports a hesitation or change of intention; close it within the available padding if it only creates a distracting flash.

## A short cue with a high reading speed

This sentence has 13 letters and 19 characters including spaces and punctuation. Over half a second, that is 26 CPS or 38 CPS respectively. A familiar expression can be recognized quickly, but 500 ms still needs review. Check whether a longer hold fits the voice and the next cue before accepting the short duration.

Try the same text in the [reading-speed example](criterios.md#try-the-count-yourself) to see how duration and counting conventions affect the result.

## A long sentence with a natural pause

A comma can suggest a split, especially when the voice pauses there. Read both halves in sequence. If one becomes an awkward fragment or a flash, keep the sentence together or choose another boundary. Punctuation alone cannot supply the missing screen time.

## A sign in the picture

Time a sign to its appearance and disappearance in the image. If it is visible too briefly, check whether the translation can extend without confusing which object it belongs to. Speech detection is not the right reference for this cue.
