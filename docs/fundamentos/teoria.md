# Start and end times

Every cue has two edges: the time it appears and the time it disappears. They need separate checks because an entry and an exit put different demands on the viewer. An entry needs to meet the speech without revealing it too soon. An exit has to preserve the last word, allow enough reading time, and fit what comes next.

There is rarely one perceptually perfect millisecond. Look for a small range that works with the sound and picture, then choose an edge for a specific reason. The available frames, the preceding cue, and the next spoken phrase narrow that range. An edge that works alone may need to move once the whole exchange is played.

## The entry meets the speech

A little lead-in usually helps the viewer find the text as the first word arrives. An entry can coincide with the first word when the surprise or force of a reply depends on its sudden arrival. Check the lead-in against the previous cue and the picture: an early appearance can reveal a reply during the pause that precedes it.

A late entry leaves the opening syllable uncovered. An early one can spoil a pause or put the words over an unrelated shot. Listen to the onset, then play the cue with its predecessor to judge the entry in context.

## The exit allows the thought to finish

The last sound is the earliest useful reference for the exit. Reading may need longer, especially for unfamiliar names, dense phrasing, or text that competes with action on screen. The exit generally benefits from more padding than the entry. Check that extra time against the next cue and any nearby cut.

An exit is too short if it clips a word or removes the text before it can be read. It is too long if the thought has finished and the text hangs over a new action. A cut can provide a good exit once the speech and reading are already covered.

## Read the cue with its neighbors

A cue can look well timed in isolation and still make a conversation stutter. Look one cue back and one ahead. A tiny gap may create a flash of empty screen; a longer pause may be part of the performance. A speaker change or a new shot can justify a separation that would feel awkward elsewhere.

## Reading effort at the same CPS {#equal-cps-does-not-mean-equal-effort}

CPS measures text length against duration. Vocabulary, syntax, familiarity, visual line length, and competing action also affect reading.

Both counts include spaces and punctuation, excluding the surrounding quotation marks. The longer sentence already receives more time at the same CPS, yet its information may still need a second look in a busy scene. Use the number to find possible reading problems, then read the actual cue in playback.

If more duration would make the subtitle linger, reconsider the wording or [segmentation](segmentacion.md).

## Let the picture inform the decision

A cut is useful when it also marks a change of thought, speaker, or focus. A sentence that continues across a cut can stay in one cue. Splitting a coherent thought just to contain each subtitle within a shot can make it harder to follow.

Before accepting an edge, play the passage with the surrounding cues. The change should improve sync or reading without cutting information or creating an awkward appearance in the next shot.
