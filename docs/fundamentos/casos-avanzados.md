# Timing exceptions

Some scenes need a longer hold, a shared exit, or a split inside an unfinished sentence. The adjustment depends on what the viewer needs to hear, read, and discover at that moment.

## Fades and transitions

A subtitle with a fade needs time for the effect as well as for reading. In the underwater clip, “Cuando cierro los ojos...” (“When I close my eyes…”) gradually disappears while the shot continues. Extra duration allows the reader to finish before the text becomes too faint. Counting the entire event as equally readable would overstate the time available.

Check the fade at playback speed and adjust the hold within the room left by the next cue. In ASS, check the fade duration as well: extending the event and extending its fade produce different amounts of fully visible reading time.

## Brief reactions and vocal sounds

Two quick expressions from the same speaker can read more smoothly in one cue. Here, “¡Jamás! ¡No lo aceptaré!” (“No! I won't accept it!”) forms one emphatic refusal. Splitting off the first exclamation would add a brief text change within that same outburst. Separate reactions when a new speaker, intention, or distinct pause makes each one its own moment.

Their function determines how laughs, gasps, and spoken interjections are timed. A spoken “Oh” can deserve a separate cue if it marks a reaction before the next line. A laugh omitted from a dialogue translation need not extend that line's timing. If a sound is described in the subtitle, its timing follows the audible reaction. A laugh may also affect how the next words are understood, so hear the whole exchange before choosing the boundary.

## Reveal the sentence as the scene unfolds

In the ballroom clip, “En caso contrario,” (“Otherwise,”) introduces a threat. The first shot shows the speaker's feet; the camera then reveals her as she names the event and its host. The accusation about conduct in the royal garden comes later. Putting the whole threat on screen at the first word would let the reader discover it before the performance delivers it.

Keeping the opening condition on its own lets the next part arrive with the following shot. The later division reserves the accusation for its spoken turn. “Otherwise,” is grammatically dependent, yet it has a clear dramatic function here: it makes the audience wait for the consequence.

## Match the phrasing to the spoken rhythm

A compact translation may finish a thought on screen while the speaker is still building toward it. Redistributing or rephrasing the text can help it follow those spoken units while preserving the meaning, register, and sequence of information. Matching the source's syllable count does not guarantee a matching rhythm.

In this clip, the first girl begins a reply while holding her phone. “¡Espera!” (“Wait!”) interrupts the action. “No me recomiendes.” (“Don't recommend me.”) then supplies the objection, followed by the other girl's surprised question. Giving the objection to the reader with “Wait!” would remove that interval of uncertainty.

That split only helps if the hesitation is distinct and both cues remain readable; otherwise, keep “But it was already too late.” together across the cut. The ellipsis represents the hesitation heard in the voice. A shot change alone gives no reason to add one.

## Simultaneous voices and fading tails

Overlapping speakers can keep separate starts and ends. A shared exit helps when it clears the group for the next cue or meets a cut that both lines should respect. Each voice can be adjusted only as far as its words and reading time allow.

In the first clip, the complaint appears before “¡Ayase, rápido!” (“Ayase, hurry!”). Both then clear for the longer reply while the rooftop shot continues. Their common exit organizes the subtitle change within the shot. If a similar exchange ends on a scene cut, that cut can supply the shared boundary.

## A cue that stays too long

A cue can exceed the guide's review threshold when a long utterance needs that time and reads naturally as a whole. First check where the excess comes from. Unused lead-out can be shortened; a sentence still being spoken may benefit from a division between clauses. Shorter wording helps a dense translation, but cannot fix text left on screen long after its purpose has ended.

For inherited timing, compare several clear speech intervals before estimating the usual padding. The [algorithm notes](../algoritmos/index.md#estimating-padding-in-an-existing-timing-pass) explain how the median and dispersion help separate a recurring choice from an exceptional cue.
